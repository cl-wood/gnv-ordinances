# ImagineGNV Comprehensive Plan: Housing Barriers and Recommended Improvements

This document identifies provisions in the ImagineGNV comprehensive plan that create barriers to housing construction and recommends changes to increase housing supply.

---

## 1. Special Use Permit Requirements for Height Increases

### [SUP Requirements Add Cost and Uncertainty to Building Taller]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.1.12 defines multiple land use categories that require a Special Use Permit (SUP) to exceed base height limits: Mixed-Use Low-Intensity (MUL) and Mixed-Use Medium-Intensity (MUM) allow 5 stories base, up to 8 with SUP; Urban Mixed-Use (UMU) allows 4-5 stories base, up to 6-7 with bonus; Office (O) and Commercial (C) allow 5 stories base, up to 8 with SUP.

**Problem:** Special Use Permits add time, cost, and uncertainty to the development process. They require discretionary approval, which can deter housing construction. Developers cannot predict whether a SUP will be approved, making it harder to finance projects and plan construction timelines.

**Proposed replacement:** "Height increases in MUL, MUM, Office, and Commercial categories up to 8 stories shall be permitted by-right without requiring a Special Use Permit. Height bonuses shall be granted administratively when developments meet objective criteria such as affordable housing inclusion or transit proximity."

---

## 2. Low Density Maximums in Single-Family Category

### [12 Units Per Acre Cap Limits Housing Variety]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.1.12 defines the Single Family (SF) land use category as allowing "up to 12 units per acre."

**Problem:** Limiting single-family areas to 12 units per acre prevents the construction of duplexes, triplexes, and small apartment buildings that could provide more affordable housing options in established neighborhoods. This forces new housing to the outskirts of the city where land is cheaper but transit and services are scarce.

**Proposed replacement:** "Single Family (SF) land use shall allow up to 20 units per acre. Missing middle housing types including duplexes, triplexes, fourplexes, and cottage courts shall be permitted by-right in all SF zones without requiring rezoning."

---

## 3. Minimum Density Requirements

### [Minimum Density Mandates Block Small Projects]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.1.12 establishes minimum density requirements in several land use categories including Residential Medium-Density (RM), Residential High-Density (RH), Mixed-Use Low-Intensity (MUL), Mixed-Use Medium-Intensity (MUM), and Urban Mixed-Use High-Intensity (UMUH). Exemptions exist only for lots 0.5 acres or smaller.

**Problem:** Minimum density requirements can prevent incremental development, increase financing complexity, and discourage small-scale infill projects. A property owner who wants to build a duplex may be forced to build a larger project or nothing at all, reducing overall housing production.

**Proposed replacement:** "Minimum density requirements are eliminated in all land use categories. Developments may proceed at any density up to the maximum permitted density without penalty or additional review."

---

## 4. Idylwild/Serenola Special Area Restrictions

### [88-Unit Cap and Single-Family Mandate Lock in Low Density]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.5.8 states that 44 acres in the Idylwild/Serenola Special Area "shall be permitted to be developed with no more than 88 residential units, each of which must be single-family and detached." Adjacent properties are limited to a "2 du/a increase in density above the density permitted on any adjacent land."

**Problem:** These restrictions lock in extremely low density (2 units per acre) in an area that could accommodate more housing. Requiring all units to be "single-family and detached" prohibits townhomes, duplexes, and other efficient housing types. The 2 du/acre adjacency step-up perpetuates low density across the entire area.

**Proposed replacement:** "The 88-unit cap is removed. Development in the Idylwild/Serenola Special Area shall be permitted consistent with underlying zoning designations. Attached housing types including townhomes and duplexes are permitted. Adjacent properties may be developed at densities up to 6 du/acre above neighboring density."

---

## 5. Adjacency/Transition Requirements

### [Buffer and Transition Rules Reduce Buildable Area]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.3.1 requires "separating high-intensity uses from low-intensity uses with transitional uses and by buffering of adjacent uses with landscape, building type, and site design." Policy 1.5.8b limits adjacent properties to "not higher than a 2 du/a increase in density above the density permitted on any adjacent land."

**Problem:** These buffering and transition requirements reduce developable area, limit density on sites adjacent to single-family zones, and create de facto exclusionary zones around low-density areas. A site that could support 50 units might only be allowed 20 because of what's next door.

**Proposed replacement:** "Compatibility between adjacent uses shall be achieved through design standards including landscaping, building orientation, and setbacks. Density on any site shall be determined by the site's own land use designation, not by adjacent properties. The 2 du/acre adjacency limit is eliminated."

---

## 6. Residential Component Requirements in Mixed-Use Zones

### [Mandatory 10% Residential Complicates Mixed-Use Development]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.1.12 states that in Mixed-Use Low-Intensity (MUL) and Mixed-Use Medium-Intensity (MUM) categories, land development regulations "shall encourage that: at least 10 percent of the floor area of new development or redevelopment of such sites be residential" for sites 10 acres or larger.

**Problem:** Mandatory residential components complicate development financing, add construction complexity (residential and commercial have different building codes), and deter commercial development that could later convert to mixed-use when market conditions are right.

**Proposed replacement:** "The 10% residential requirement for developments on sites 10 acres or larger is converted to a voluntary incentive. Developments that include residential components may receive density bonuses or expedited permitting. Alternatively, developments may pay an in-lieu fee toward the City's affordable housing fund."

---

## 7. Inclusionary Zoning Threshold

### [50-Unit Threshold Creates Cliff Effect]

**Current policy:** The plan references inclusionary zoning changes where "new development of 50 units or more within certain zoning districts must provide some of the units as affordable housing."

**Problem:** The 50-unit threshold creates a cliff effect where developers may build 49 units to avoid the requirement, resulting in smaller buildings than sites can support, lost housing supply, and fragmented development patterns. A developer might split a 70-unit project into two 35-unit projects to avoid the requirement entirely.

**Proposed replacement:** "Inclusionary zoning shall apply on a graduated scale: developments of 10-24 units shall provide 5% affordable units, 25-49 units shall provide 10% affordable units, and 50+ units shall provide 15% affordable units. Developments may alternatively pay an in-lieu fee. Meaningful offsets including fee waivers, additional height allowances, and reduced parking requirements shall be provided to offset compliance costs."

---

## 8. Parking Requirements

### [Parking Mandates Increase Costs and Reduce Housing]

**Current policy:** While Policy 2.1.4 mentions "continuing to reduce or eliminate parking requirements," the plan still regulates parking: Policy 1.4.5 regulates "location and amount of parking," Policy 1.4.6 continues to "regulate subdivision of land, vehicle parking, and on-site traffic flow," and Policy 4.2.8 requires bicycle parking as part of development.

**Problem:** Parking requirements increase construction costs (each parking space costs $20,000-$50,000 to build), reduce buildable area, and discourage housing near transit where parking is least needed. Parking mandates effectively subsidize car ownership at the expense of housing affordability.

**Proposed replacement:** "All minimum parking requirements are eliminated citywide. Developers may provide parking based on market demand. Maximum parking limits shall be established in transit-rich areas to prevent excessive surface parking. Bicycle parking requirements are retained to encourage sustainable transportation."

---

## 9. Review and Permitting Process Complexity

### [Multi-Stage Reviews Add Time and Cost]

**Current policy:** The plan requires multiple review processes: Policy 5.1.7 establishes concurrency management system reviews to determine "whether adequate facilities exist," and Policy 1.2.2 requires "Traffic Impact Analysis" for development plan review. Multiple approval stages include development orders, building permits, and certificates of occupancy.

**Problem:** Complex, multi-stage review processes add time and cost to housing development. Each additional review stage can add months to project timelines and thousands of dollars in consultant fees. Uncertainty about approval outcomes makes financing more difficult and expensive.

**Proposed replacement:** "Reviews shall be consolidated into a single approval process where possible. Developments meeting objective standards shall receive by-right approval within 30 days. Maximum review timeframes shall be established (30 days for by-right, 90 days for discretionary). Reviews shall be processed in parallel rather than sequentially. Developments under 10 units shall be exempt from concurrency and traffic impact analysis requirements."

---

## 10. Concurrency Requirements

### [Infrastructure Concurrency Can Block Housing]

**Current policy:** Policies 5.1.7 through 5.1.9 state that "The City shall continue operation of its Concurrency Management System" to determine "whether adequate facilities exist" for "potable water, solid waste, stormwater management, water supply, recreation, and wastewater" before approving development.

**Problem:** Strict concurrency can block housing in areas with infrastructure deficits, shift infrastructure costs to individual developments (making projects financially infeasible), and create uncertainty in the development process. A housing project may be denied because of infrastructure deficits the developer cannot control.

**Proposed replacement:** "Infill developments on previously developed parcels are exempt from concurrency requirements. Other developments may proceed with proportionate share contributions toward infrastructure improvements rather than requiring infrastructure to be in place. The City shall establish a concurrency bank allowing developers to pay fees in lieu of infrastructure construction, with the City prioritizing infrastructure investment in designated growth areas."

---

## 11. Tree Canopy and Environmental Requirements

### [Tree Surveys and Cluster Mandates Limit Buildable Area]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.5.3 states that "development regulations shall require a tree survey be submitted for all development proposed within designated 'Tree Canopy Areas'" and that "development within Tree Canopy Areas shall utilize 'cluster' design concepts."

**Problem:** Tree surveys and cluster development requirements can reduce buildable area, increase site development costs through survey requirements and design constraints, and limit housing density in desirable areas. The vague "where appropriate" language adds uncertainty to the development process.

**Proposed replacement:** "Developments may satisfy tree canopy requirements through in-lieu fees that fund tree planting on public land elsewhere in the city. Green roofs and vertical landscaping shall count toward tree requirements. Clear, objective standards shall replace subjective review for tree preservation, with specific metrics for canopy coverage that developers can meet through multiple pathways."

---

## 12. Floor Area Ratio (FAR) Limits

### [FAR Caps Prevent Full Use of Building Envelopes]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.1.12 establishes that the Business Industrial (BI) category has "a maximum floor area ratio of 4.0."

**Problem:** FAR limits prevent developers from fully utilizing permitted building envelopes. A site that could support a taller building under height limits may be restricted by FAR, reducing the total housing that can be built. FAR calculations can also be manipulated to exclude affordable projects.

**Proposed replacement:** "Floor area ratio limits are eliminated in all mixed-use, commercial, and industrial zones. Building intensity shall be controlled through height limits and setbacks only, allowing developers to maximize the utilization of permitted building envelopes."

---

## 13. Missing "By-Right" Multi-Family in More Zones

### [Multi-Family Restricted to Limited Zoning Categories]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.1.12 limits multi-family housing to specific zoning designations. The Single Family (SF) category allows only "Single-Family Detached" plus ADUs. Residential Low-Density (RL) allows only "Small-Scale Multi-Family." Full multi-family requires Residential Medium-Density (RM), Residential High-Density (RH), or mixed-use designations.

**Problem:** Restricting multi-family housing to limited zoning categories creates artificial scarcity in most of the city's residential land. Property owners in SF or RL zones cannot build apartments even when there is strong housing demand, concentrating new housing in a few areas and driving up prices.

**Proposed replacement:** "Small-scale multi-family (4-8 units) shall be permitted by-right in all Residential Low-Density (RL) zones. Medium-scale multi-family (up to 20 units) shall be permitted by-right on parcels within 1/4 mile of transit corridors regardless of underlying zoning. Conversion of single-family homes to multi-family shall be permitted without rezoning in all residential zones."

---

## 14. Mixed-Use Office/Residential (MOR) Height Limit

### [3-Story Maximum in Mixed-Use Office/Residential]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.1.12 defines the Mixed-Use Office/Residential (MOR) land use category with: "Maximum building height shall be 3 stories."

**Problem:** A strict 3-story cap severely limits housing potential in areas designated for mixed residential and office use. This height restriction prevents the construction of mid-rise apartment buildings that could provide substantial housing near employment centers, forcing development to sprawl outward instead of building upward.

**Proposed replacement:** "Maximum building height shall be 5 stories by-right, with the option to increase to 8 stories through administrative approval when meeting affordable housing or transit proximity criteria."

---

## 15. Urban Mixed-Use (UMU) Height Restrictions

### [Low Height Limits in Urban Mixed-Use Areas]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.1.12 defines the Urban Mixed-Use (UMU) category where buildings "shall not exceed 4 to 5 stories, depending upon the implementing zoning district, with provisions to add up to an additional 1 to 2 stories by a height bonus system."

**Problem:** Urban mixed-use areas are specifically designated for dense, walkable development, yet the 4-5 story base height with only 1-2 bonus stories limits buildings to a maximum of 6-7 stories. This wastes the potential of well-located urban land that could support significantly more housing.

**Proposed replacement:** "Base building height shall be 8 stories, with the option to increase to 12 stories through a by-right density bonus for developments meeting affordable housing, sustainability, or transit-oriented development criteria."

---

## 16. Urban Mixed-Use High Intensity (UMUH) Height Cap

### [Arbitrary 8-Story Maximum in High-Intensity Mixed-Use]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.1.12 defines the Urban Mixed-Use High-Intensity (UMUH) category where "Building height shall be limited to 6 stories and up to 8 stories by a height bonus system."

**Problem:** Despite being designated as "high-intensity" and specifically intended to support development near the University of Florida, this category only allows a maximum of 8 stories. This artificially constrains housing supply in one of the city's most transit-rich and walkable areas where taller buildings would be appropriate.

**Proposed replacement:** "Base building height shall be 10 stories, with the option to increase to 16 stories through a by-right density bonus for developments meeting affordable housing criteria. No Special Use Permit shall be required for height bonuses."

---

## 17. Urban Core (UC) Height Limitations

### [Downtown Height Cap Below Optimal Density]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.1.12 defines the Urban Core (UC) category where buildings are limited to "12 stories, with up to 14 stories by a height bonus systems."

**Problem:** The urban core is the most appropriate location in any city for tall buildings and high-density housing. Capping downtown buildings at 12-14 stories prevents Gainesville from achieving the housing density that would support vibrant urban life, reduce housing costs through supply, and maximize transit and infrastructure investments.

**Proposed replacement:** "There shall be no maximum building height in the Urban Core. Building height shall be regulated only by FAA requirements, building code structural standards, and shadow/daylight requirements where applicable."

---

## 18. Conservation and Agriculture Density Restrictions

### [Extremely Low Density in Conservation and Agriculture Zones]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.1.12 defines the Conservation (CON) land use category allowing "single-family densities of 1 unit per 5 acres" for privately held properties. The Agriculture (AGR) category similarly allows "single-family at densities no greater than densities of 1 unit per 5 acres."

**Problem:** While conservation and agricultural lands serve important purposes, privately held lands within city limits in these categories are locked into extremely low densities (1 unit per 5 acres = 0.2 units per acre). This effectively prohibits housing development on these parcels even when they may be appropriate for housing due to changed circumstances.

**Proposed replacement:** "For privately held Conservation or Agriculture parcels within city limits that are not subject to conservation easements or environmental protections, the City shall establish a streamlined process to redesignate these lands for residential use at densities appropriate to surrounding development patterns."

---

## 19. Mandatory Sewer Connection Requirements

### [Infrastructure Requirements Blocking Development]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.5.10e states that new development in the Idylwild/Serenola area is prohibited "unless it is connected to a public water supply and a centralized sewer facility" with limited exceptions only for single-family homes on lots of 3 acres or more.

**Problem:** Requiring connection to centralized sewer can block housing development in areas where sewer lines have not yet been extended, even when modern on-site treatment systems could safely serve new homes. The 3-acre minimum for exceptions also prevents smaller-lot development.

**Proposed replacement:** "New development shall connect to centralized sewer where available. Where centralized sewer is not available, development may proceed with approved on-site wastewater treatment systems that meet state environmental standards, regardless of lot size."

---

## 20. Wetland Buffer Requirements

### [Excessive Wetland Setbacks Reducing Buildable Area]

**Current policy:** Policy 3.3.1 (in the Conservation chapter) requires "a minimum buffer distance of 35 feet and an average buffer distance of 75 feet from the landward extent of any wetland" and states that "larger buffers may be warranted."

**Problem:** While wetland protection is important, inflexible buffer requirements of 35-75 feet or more can significantly reduce buildable area on parcels near wetlands, making housing development financially infeasible. The vague "larger buffers may be warranted" language adds uncertainty.

**Proposed replacement:** "Wetland buffers shall be a minimum of 25 feet, with flexibility to reduce buffers to 15 feet when developments incorporate compensating stormwater management features or green infrastructure. Buffer averaging shall be permitted to allow reasonable site design while maintaining overall wetland protection."

---

## 21. Traffic Impact Analysis Requirements

### [Traffic Studies Adding Cost and Delay]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.2.2 (in the Transportation chapter) requires "Traffic Impact Analysis" for "comprehensive plan amendments, campus master plans, special area studies and site access studies for development plan review."

**Problem:** Traffic impact analysis requirements add cost and time to the development process. While transportation planning is important, applying these requirements broadly can delay housing projects and add engineering costs that ultimately increase housing prices.

**Proposed replacement:** "Traffic Impact Analysis shall only be required for developments exceeding 200 residential units or generating more than 1,000 daily vehicle trips. Developments below these thresholds shall be subject only to standard site access review. Infill developments within 1/4 mile of transit shall be exempt from traffic impact analysis requirements."

---

## 22. Stormwater Treatment Requirements

### [Stormwater Mandates Increasing Development Costs]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.1.3 (in the Stormwater chapter) states "The City shall require stormwater quality treatment facilities for development" and regulates impervious surfaces including "removing minimum parking requirements and reducing the maximum allowed parking where appropriate."

**Problem:** While stormwater management is necessary, requiring each development to construct its own treatment facilities increases costs and reduces buildable area. Small infill projects are particularly burdened by these requirements.

**Proposed replacement:** "Small infill developments of 10 units or fewer on previously developed lots shall be exempt from on-site stormwater treatment requirements, provided they do not increase impervious surface area beyond pre-development conditions. The City shall establish a stormwater fee-in-lieu program allowing developments to contribute to regional stormwater facilities rather than constructing on-site facilities."

---

## 23. Industrial (IND) Height Limit

### [5-Story Cap on Industrial Land]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.1.12 defines the Industrial (IND) land use category where "Intensity will be controlled by adopting land development regulations that establish height limits of 5 stories or less."

**Problem:** Industrial areas are increasingly being converted to mixed-use and residential development as cities evolve. The 5-story height limit prevents adaptive reuse of industrial parcels for housing at densities that could make redevelopment financially viable.

**Proposed replacement:** "Industrial parcels may be developed or redeveloped for residential or mixed-use purposes at heights up to 8 stories by-right when consistent with surrounding development patterns, or up to 12 stories when located within 1/2 mile of transit stops or employment centers."

---

## 24. Business Industrial (BI) FAR and Height Limits

### [Restrictions on Business Industrial Development]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.1.12 defines the Business Industrial (BI) category with "a maximum height of 5 stories, and a maximum floor area ratio of 4.0" when not within an airport zone of influence.

**Problem:** The combined height and FAR restrictions limit the intensity of development in business industrial areas, preventing these well-located parcels (often near transportation infrastructure) from accommodating housing through mixed-use redevelopment.

**Proposed replacement:** "Business Industrial parcels outside airport zones of influence may be developed for mixed-use including residential at heights up to 10 stories with no FAR limitation, subject to building code and design standards."

---

## 25. Secondary Use Restrictions in Residential High-Density

### [25% Cap on Commercial in High-Density Residential]

**Current policy:** Policy 1.1.12 defines the Residential High-Density (RH) category where "The intensity of secondary retail and office use cannot exceed 25% of the residential floor area."

**Problem:** Limiting commercial uses to 25% of residential floor area prevents the development of truly mixed-use buildings where residents can live above shops, restaurants, and services. This restriction forces commercial uses to be built separately, reducing walkability and convenience.

**Proposed replacement:** "Secondary retail, office, and service uses are encouraged in Residential High-Density areas and may occupy up to 50% of ground floor area in buildings of 4 or more stories."

## Summary of Key Recommendations

| Barrier | Recommendation |
|---------|----------------|
| SUP for height | By-right height increases with objective criteria |
| Low SF density | Allow 18-20 units/acre; permit missing middle housing |
| Minimum density | Eliminate or significantly reduce minimums |
| Idylwild/Serenola caps | Remove unit caps; allow attached housing |
| Adjacency transitions | Replace density limits with design standards |
| Mixed-use residential requirement | Make optional or allow in-lieu fees |
| IZ threshold at 50 units | Lower threshold with graduated scale |
| Parking minimums | Eliminate; set maximums instead |
| Complex review process | Consolidate; establish by-right approvals |
| Concurrency requirements | Exempt infill; allow proportionate share |
| Tree/environmental requirements | Allow in-lieu fees; clear standards |
| FAR limits | Eliminate in favor of height/setback controls |
| Limited multi-family zones | Expand by-right multi-family permissions |
| MOR 3-story limit | Increase to 5 stories by-right, 8 with bonus |
| UMU 4-5 story limit | Increase to 8 stories by-right, 12 with bonus |
| UMUH 6-8 story limit | Increase to 10 stories by-right, 16 with bonus |
| Urban Core 12-14 story limit | Eliminate height cap downtown |
| Conservation/Agriculture density | Create redesignation pathway |
| Mandatory sewer connection | Allow modern on-site systems |
| Wetland buffers | Reduce minimums; allow flexibility |
| Traffic impact analysis | Exempt small/infill projects |
| Stormwater requirements | Create fee-in-lieu; exempt small infill |
| Industrial 5-story limit | Allow 8-12 stories for residential reuse |
| Business Industrial limits | Remove FAR cap; allow 10-story mixed-use |
| RH commercial cap | Increase to 50% of ground floor |

## Conclusion

The ImagineGNV comprehensive plan contains language supportive of housing production, including Policy 1.1.1 which calls for identifying and removing barriers that "limit the construction of new housing" or "unnecessarily increase the cost of creating housing." The plan also acknowledges that "exclusionary zoning regulations like large lot sizes, wide setbacks, and low densities directly limit housing supply."

However, the specific land use categories and policies contain numerous provisions that contradict these goals. The plan includes at least 25 distinct categories of restrictions that limit housing supply through:

- **Height limits** in virtually every land use category, even in areas designated for urban intensity
- **Density caps** that prevent efficient use of land
- **Process requirements** (SUPs, traffic studies, concurrency reviews) that add time and cost
- **Environmental mandates** (buffers, stormwater, tree preservation) applied inflexibly
- **Infrastructure requirements** that can block development entirely

Implementing the recommendations above would better align the comprehensive plan with its stated objectives of ensuring "diverse and abundant housing" for all Gainesville residents. The key principle should be: **allow more housing by-right, with clear and objective standards, rather than through discretionary review processes that add cost, time, and uncertainty.**
